Undescended Testicles

Undescended testicles (also known as cryptorchidism) is a condition in which one or both of a male infant's testicles fail to descend into the scrotum before birth. Normally, the testicles develop inside the abdomen during fetal life and descend into the scrotum in the final months of pregnancy. If the testicles do not descend on their own by the time of birth or shortly thereafter, the condition is diagnosed as undescended testicles. This condition can lead to complications such as infertility, hormonal imbalances, or increased risk of testicular cancer if left untreated.

Physiology of the Testicles

The testicles (or testes) are responsible for the production of sperm and the hormone testosterone. They begin developing in the abdomen during fetal life and, in the last trimester of pregnancy, they typically descend into the scrotum, which is located outside the body. This descent is essential for proper sperm production, as the scrotum provides a cooler environment that is necessary for optimal sperm function. If a testicle remains undescended, it may experience abnormal temperature regulation, leading to impaired sperm production and potential fertility issues in the future.

Causes of Undescended Testicles

The causes of undescended testicles are not always clear, but several factors can contribute to this condition:

  • Genetic Factors: In some cases, undescended testicles may run in families, suggesting a genetic predisposition. Certain genetic syndromes, such as Down syndrome, may also be associated with undescended testicles.
  • Prematurity: Babies born prematurely (before 37 weeks of gestation) are at higher risk of having undescended testicles because the testicles may not have had enough time to descend fully during the pregnancy.
  • Hormonal Imbalances: Hormonal deficiencies or imbalances, such as low levels of testosterone or other reproductive hormones, may interfere with the normal descent of the testicles.
  • Intra-abdominal Pressure: Conditions that affect intra-abdominal pressure, such as a hernia, may prevent the testicles from descending into the scrotum.
  • Environmental Factors: Exposure to certain environmental factors, such as endocrine-disrupting chemicals, may interfere with the normal development and descent of the testicles during fetal life.
  • Structural Abnormalities: Rarely, anatomical problems in the inguinal canal or abdominal cavity may block the path of the testicles' descent.
